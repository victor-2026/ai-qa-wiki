# Tony Seale – Jev, Ontologies and the Neural‑Symbolic Loop (2024‑09‑25)

## Summary
On 15 September 2024 the company **Jev** released a judgment‑as‑a‑service platform that separates *definition work* from *fast inference*.  The service receives a structured request (data + criteria + answer schema) and returns a typed verdict together with calibrated probabilities.  Pricing is $0.042 per million input tokens, output is free, and latency ranges from 70 ms to 500 ms.  

Seale argues that the economic advantage of Jev stems from **ontologies** (formal specifications of classes, exclusions, hierarchies and vocabularies) and **knowledge graphs** (the evidence store).  The ontology supplies the *meaning* of each decision category, while the graph supplies the concrete facts that the model must judge.  The two layers form a **neural‑symbolic loop**: slow, human‑driven ontology maintenance on one side, and rapid neural judgment on the other.

## Key Concepts

| Concept | What it means | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Ontology as specification** | Formalises judgment criteria: <br>• *Class definitions* → concrete classification rules <br>• *Disjointness* → mutually exclusive outcomes <br>• *Hierarchy* → organized choice sets <br>• *Vocabulary levels* → granularity of scales | Guarantees that every fast verdict is grounded in a precise, reusable definition, reducing ambiguity and token‑costs. |
| **Graph as evidence store** | Stores business artefacts (tickets, services, contracts, incidents) as linked nodes.  When a request arrives, the relevant sub‑graph is assembled and handed to Jev. | Provides the factual substrate the model needs; the model never “re‑creates” business knowledge, it merely evaluates it. |
| **Neural‑symbolic loop** | *System One*: Jev’s low‑latency neural judgment (no deliberation). <br>*System Two*: Ongoing ontology/graph curation and human oversight. | Enables continuous synchronisation: updates to definitions instantly affect future judgments, while model behaviour informs ontology refinement. |
| **Calibration‑repays‑definitions** | Near‑50/50 probability outputs flag vague or under‑specified criteria. | Turns calibration signals into a quality‑control metric for specification documents. |
| **Spec‑mapping table** | Direct mapping of ontology constructs to judgment‑spec elements (class ↔ criterion, disjointness ↔ exclusion, hierarchy ↔ choice set, vocab level ↔ scale). | Serves as a checklist for authoring robust decision criteria that survive the neural‑symbolic loop. |
| **Evidence pattern (ticket → service → contract → incidents)** | A reusable template for assembling the context that Jev must evaluate. | Guarantees that the model receives all relevant facts without needing to infer missing links. |
| **System One/Two runtime split** | Fast inference (70‑500 ms) vs. slow definitional work (ontology/graph updates). | Aligns cost structures: cheap, high‑throughput judgments rely on expensive, high‑quality definitions. |

## Practical Applications

1. **Vagueness detection for internal specifications**  
   - Monitor Jev’s probability distribution.  
   - Flag verdicts with confidence ≈ 50 % as candidates for spec review, not just human escalation.  

2. **Checklist for authoring judgment criteria**  
   - Use the spec‑mapping table to ensure every new decision rule includes a clear class definition, explicit exclusions, a bounded hierarchy, and an appropriate scale.  
   - Pair with CIGE (stable intent) and risk‑tier gating to embed the rule into the decision pipeline.  

3. **Context‑assembly template**  
   - When designing a new service‑level‑agreement (SLA) check, populate the ticket → service → contract → incidents chain.  
   - Feed the assembled sub‑graph to Jev, letting the ontology interpret each node’s meaning.  

4. **Road‑mapping and monitoring**  
   - Track Jev’s launch milestones (Sep 15 release, HN thread ≈ 2000 points, pricing, latency) as reference points for future neural‑symbolic products.  
   - Use the timeline to benchmark latency, cost, and calibration improvements across releases.  

5. **Economic token‑saving**  
   - By defining categories once in the ontology and re‑using graph facts, each judgment avoids re‑encoding business logic, mirroring the token‑saving principle highlighted in Seale’s earlier work.
