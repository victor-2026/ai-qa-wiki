## Summary  
Alternate QA released a prototype “Test Automation Architect Simulator” that puts a manager through six virtual quarters of a software‑delivery organization. Teams report that their automation is healthy; the architect must decide which claims to trust, allocate remediation resources, and keep sponsor confidence while uncovering hidden blockers. The idea originated with Bas Dijkstra and is being refined after feedback from Paul Kanaris, who stresses that the biggest impediments to automation maturity often lie **outside** the automation practice itself (requirements quality, organizational incentives, culture, etc.).  

---

## Key Concepts  

| Concept | Description | Relevance to the Simulator |
|---------|-------------|----------------------------|
| **Believed vs Confirmed** | The gap between what teams claim and what is actually verified. | Core game mechanic; mirrors the “evidence doctrine” (claims vs verified verdicts). |
| **Sponsor Trust** | Stakeholder confidence that gaps will be closed on schedule. | Measured in the sim; drives “attestation economics” in miniature. |
| **Unchecked Assumptions** | Risks that remain untested until they cause a blocker. | Players must fund verification; the cost/impact model is a central tension. |
| **External Constraints** (Kanaris thesis) | Factors such as requirements clarity, product understanding, delivery pressure, and culture that affect automation outcomes more than tooling. | Currently only lightly represented; slated for the next iteration. |
| **Mutation‑Matrix / Seeded Doubt** | A design pattern where simulated doubts are injected and must be resolved through verification. | Provides a teaching format for risk‑tier thinking. |

---

## Practical Applications  

1. **Training New Architects** – The simulator offers a safe environment to practice distinguishing between optimistic self‑reports and hard evidence, reinforcing evidence‑based decision‑making.  
2. **Evidence Doctrine Alignment** – By mapping “believed‑vs‑confirmed” to claims vs verified verdicts, teams can rehearse attestation processes before applying them to real projects.  
3. **Organizational Diagnosis** – Incorporating Kanaris’s external‑constraint lens helps trainees recognize that bottlenecks often stem from requirements, incentives, or culture, prompting broader stakeholder analysis.  
4. **Curriculum Development** – The mutation‑matrix concept can be turned into classroom exercises, illustrating how verification cost and blocker risk interact.  
5. **Continuous Improvement Loop** – Feedback from early users (e.g., Kanaris) directs feature prioritization, ensuring the simulator evolves to cover the full spectrum of quality constraints.
