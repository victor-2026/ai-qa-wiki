# Responsibility Is the Human Moat – Principles of Responsible Work v2.0  

*Authors: James Bach, Jon Bach, Michael Bolton*  
*Source: Satisfice blog (2026‑05‑20)*  

## Summary  
AI tools can simulate participation in a business, but they lack legal standing and cannot bear responsibility. The “human moat” is the gap that only natural persons can cross: only a competent, accountable person may be held liable for a service’s safety and reliability. The updated Principles of Responsible Work (v2.0) spell out how businesses must structure responsibility around people, not tools, and describe the specific hazards that arise when AI is mis‑treated as an autonomous agent.

## Key Concepts  

| # | Concept | Essence |
|---|----------|---------|
| 1 | **Service reliability** | Every non‑trivial business depends on services that must work well enough to keep the enterprise alive. |
| 2 | **Risk awareness** | Failures are inevitable; organizations must detect, recover, and (in regulated domains) follow mandated risk‑management processes. |
| 3 | **Responsible person** | A natural person who is competent, prepared, and legally accountable. Children and AI lack this capacity. |
| 4 | **Responsible service** | Work performed in good faith by a responsible person: following procedures, improving skills, anticipating problems, and reporting issues. |
| 5 | **Tool‑augmented work** | Tools—including AI—are permissible only when the operator can use them safely and efficiently; complexity and output volume raise the skill burden. |
| 6 | **Responsibility handling** | Responsibility may be taken, shared, declined, or delegated, but only under a clear, reasonable protocol. |
| 7 | **AI as a tool, not an agent** | AI agents are always operated by a person; the operator retains full liability for outages, token limits, or degraded performance. |
| 8 | **Special AI hazards** | Technological (service outages, model drift), cognitive (overload, debt, atrophy, surrender, anthropomorphism, automation bias, chronic stress), and managerial (data negligence, reckless spending, legal violations, moral crumple zones, loss of tacit knowledge). |
| 9 | **AI Productivity Paradox** | Responsible AI use often consumes more time than it saves; incentives to appear productive push users toward irresponsible shortcuts. |
|10| **Quality vs. responsibility** | The central issue is not AI accuracy but who is answerable for the output. “Human‑in‑the‑loop” is an ethical prerequisite, not a fallback for low‑quality models. |

## Practical Applications  

- **Define clear ownership** – Document who is the responsible person for each service, including AI‑augmented ones, and ensure the person has the required competence and authority to reject unsatisfactory AI output.  
- **Skill‑based onboarding** – Provide training that covers prompt engineering, model limits, and failure‑mode detection; certify operators before granting production access.  
- **Risk‑aware tooling** – Choose AI services with observable SLAs, monitor token usage, and implement fallback mechanisms for outages or model drift.  
- **Cognitive load management** – Limit concurrent AI streams, schedule regular “offline” periods for manual verification, and rotate tasks to prevent debt and atrophy.  
- **Governance protocols** – Establish a delegation matrix that specifies when responsibility can be shared or transferred, and require documented hand‑offs and sign‑offs.  
- **Audit & attestation** – Integrate a “human gate” (e.g., mutation matrix review) where the operator must explicitly attest to the correctness of AI‑generated artifacts before release.  
- **Legal compliance checks** – Align AI usage with regulations such as the EU AI Act; verify that contracts do not shift liability onto the tool.  
- **Stress mitigation** – Offer counseling, reasonable workload caps, and clear escalation paths to avoid chronic stress and burnout among AI supervisors.  

## Discussion Highlights  

- Framing AI adoption as a quality problem obscures the real ethical challenge: who can be held accountable when things go wrong.  
- Even “irresponsible” services (e.g., ChatGPT with OpenAI’s disclaimer) do not relieve the employee of personal responsibility for the final work product.  
- Transparency about AI use is optional unless the operator cannot guarantee quality; in that case, disclosure becomes a risk‑mitigation measure.
