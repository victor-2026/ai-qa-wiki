## Summary  
In a recent LinkedIn post (27 Aug 2026), QA thought‑leader Ruslan Desyatnikov warns of a growing “QA Director elimination virus.” Within a single week, three senior QA leaders were removed from client organisations, a pattern that is now appearing across multiple enterprises. While the cuts are justified as short‑term cost savings, they strip companies of the people who own and safeguard product quality—especially at a time when AI‑generated code, requirements, and tests are increasingly trusted to validate each other. The danger lies in a false sense of security that can lead to production failures, security breaches, regulatory penalties, and reputational harm.

---

## Key Concepts  

| Concept | Explanation |
|---------|-------------|
| **Leadership vacuum** | Removing QA directors eliminates accountability, not the need for quality. The remaining teams lack a clear owner for the final quality verdict. |
| **AI‑centric validation loop** | AI tools now produce code, requirements, and tests that cross‑validate each other. If the AI’s underlying model is flawed, the same error can propagate through all artefacts, remaining invisible until release. |
| **Short‑term vs. long‑term cost** | Spreadsheet‑level savings from staff cuts are attractive, but hidden costs emerge later as incidents, security breaches, compliance failures, and loss of customer trust. |
| **Systemic pattern** | The simultaneous removal of QA leaders across several clients suggests a broader industry trend rather than isolated decisions. |
| **Responsibility disclaimer** | Executives who dismiss warnings risk being held accountable when failures materialise (“Do NOT say later that I did NOT warn you”). |

## Practical Applications  

1. **Maintain a QA Ownership Model**  
   - Define a clear “quality champion” role that remains even when AI tools are used.  
   - Document decision‑making authority for release sign‑off.  

2. **Implement Independent Verification**  
   - Use a separate, preferably human‑centric, validation layer to audit AI‑generated artefacts.  
   - Apply mutation testing or adversarial testing to expose hidden AI biases.  

3. **Cost‑Benefit Analysis with Risk Weighting**  
   - Extend financial models to include potential incident costs, regulatory fines, and brand impact.  
   - Adopt a “total cost of ownership” view that factors in post‑release remediation.  

4. **Governance Framework for AI‑Driven QA**  
   - Establish policies that require traceability from AI‑generated requirements through to production.  
   - Enforce periodic external audits to ensure compliance with industry standards (e.g., ISO 25010, SOC 2).  

5. **Executive Education**  
   - Conduct workshops for C‑level leaders highlighting the limits of AI confidence scores.  
   - Share case studies of AI‑induced failures to illustrate long‑term risks.  

By preserving dedicated QA leadership, integrating independent checks, and embedding risk‑aware governance, organisations can reap AI benefits without sacrificing product integrity.
