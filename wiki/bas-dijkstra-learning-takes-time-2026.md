# Learning Takes Time — But Do We Get That Time?

**Source:** On Test Automation newsletter (Bas Dijkstra, bas@ontestautomation.com)
**Date:** September 7, 2026 (via user email; no public URL)
**Tags:** #craftsmanship #test-automation #junior-qa #learning #AI-hiring

## What It Is

Bas Dijkstra's newsletter essay on the disappearing space for learning the test automation craft. Trigger: a conversation at a contract testing workshop in the Netherlands turned to AI's effect on the craft and on hiring, especially juniors. His thesis: experience took him 20 years to build; companies now offer fewer and fewer such years to anyone.

## Core Argument

Test automation is a **craft** (designing, writing, running, maintaining tests — not just wielding a tool or two). Doing it well means deeply understanding what needs doing, why, and how — all converging on one goal: **automation that provides valuable feedback, fast**.

That understanding took Dijkstra 20 years of learning, doing, failing, analyzing, discussing, more failing. He was lucky: employers who gave learning time, means to learn on his own dime, clients who let him learn on theirs.

Now the opportunities shrink: traineeships on hold, no junior hiring, same time for more work. Everyone agrees not hiring juniors is unsustainable long-term — but what are we offering the juniors we do have? Space, time, opportunities to acquire craft skills?

His closing frame: someday he stops (per client, several times a year; altogether, once). Others will be asked to do his work — and they can only do it if they got to learn, like he did. AI doing parts of the work faster doesn't remove the need to learn; "we need to invest more in learning, not less."

## Key Quotes

- "I consider myself a craftsman, and I consider the work that I'm involved in to be a craft."
- "It all comes down to striving for our automation to provide valuable feedback, fast."
- "Getting that experience took years of learning, doing, failing, analyzing, discussing, more failing and more learning."
- "They can only do that if they get the opportunities to learn, just like I did."

## Why It Matters Now

- Junior QA hiring freeze across the industry meets AI agents that let one senior do the typing of five juniors — the learning ladder loses its bottom rungs exactly when the top demands new skills (steering, evals, verification).
- Direct collision with the "guided QA engineer" thesis: steering an agent requires fundamentals (Ng's list), and fundamentals require learning time. An agent-assisted junior who never got Dijkstra's 20 compressed years has nothing to steer with.
- Counterweight to pure-automation narratives: speed of generation is not speed of learning.

## Relevance to QA/QE

| Pattern | QA Application |
|---------|----------------|
| Craft = valuable feedback fast | The acceptance bar for any suite, AI-generated or not; speed without feedback value is noise |
| 20 years compressed | Per-risk-tier gates (Megi) and mutation checks are the compression mechanism: juniors learn judgment faster by reviewing agent output against real breaks |
| No junior hiring | If juniors don't get seams to own, nobody grows into the guided QA role — Article 27's pipeline runs dry |
| Invest more in learning | Human sign-off (Article 27's step d) is only trustworthy if the signer learned the craft, not just the tool |

## Critical Analysis

**Strengths:**
- First-person credibility (20 years, named context: Dutch client workshop, contract testing).
- Names the mechanism (employers' time, own dime, clients' dime) — not just nostalgia.
- Ends with a question to readers, inviting evidence over agreement.

**Gaps:**
- No data on the hiring freeze scale (anecdotal "examples are plenty").
- Doesn't address the counter-case: AI-assisted juniors possibly learning *faster* via tight agent feedback loops — the optimistic version of his own "learn on someone's dime" (the dime is now GPU time, nearly free).
- No concrete proposal for what "invest more" looks like (traineeships 2.0? agent-paired learning? gate-review apprenticeships?).
