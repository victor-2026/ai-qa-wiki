## Summary  
James Bach critiques the euphemistic “vibe‑coding” label that many testers use when they quickly cobble together AI‑generated test tools. He proposes the blunt term **“slop‑coding”** to describe a prototype that has **not** been subjected to systematic testing or verification. By openly calling a script “slop‑coded,” a tester signals a responsibility gap: the tool works for a narrow purpose, but the creator cannot reliably stand behind it in production. Once the artifact passes proper QA, the tester can simply say “I created a tool…”. The concept highlights honesty about the limits of AI‑assisted code and ties directly to the “stand‑by‑your‑work” rule in the PRW v2.0 framework.

---

## Key Concepts  

| Concept | Meaning | Relevance to QA |
|---------|---------|-----------------|
| **Vibe‑coding** | A cute, feel‑good phrase that masks the provisional nature of AI‑generated scripts. | Can create false confidence in stakeholders. |
| **Slop‑coding** | Explicit admission that a tool was assembled quickly, without thorough testing. | Makes the responsibility gap visible; aligns with ethical testing practices. |
| **Responsibility Gap** | The difference between what a tester knows about a tool’s behavior and what others assume they know. | Critical when AI lowers the barrier to produce disposable utilities. |
| **PRW v2.0 Operator Rule** | “Be ready to stand by your work.” | Slop‑coding satisfies the rule by refusing premature endorsement. |
| **Attestation of Seldom‑Findable Artifacts** | Requirement to document and certify artifacts that are not easily discoverable in the code base. | Slop‑coded scripts often fall into this category and need explicit attestation. |

*Variants* such as “slip‑coded” or “schlep‑coded” have been floated, but “slop‑coded” captures the core idea: a tool that is **sloppy by design** and **not production‑ready**.

---

## Practical Applications  

1. **Transparent Communication** – When presenting a prototype, state “I slop‑coded a memory‑leak detector” to set realistic expectations with managers, developers, and auditors.  
2. **Documentation & Governance** – Tag slop‑coded artifacts in repositories (e.g., `#slop-coded`) and maintain a short log of known limitations, test coverage gaps, and intended next steps.  
3. **Risk Management** – Use the slop‑coding label to trigger a formal review gate: the artifact cannot be promoted to CI/CD pipelines until it passes a defined validation suite.  
4. **Training & Culture** – Incorporate the term into onboarding material for AI‑augmented testing teams, reinforcing the ethic that “shiny” AI output is not automatically trustworthy.  
5. **Audit Trail** – When auditors request evidence of testing, the slop‑coding tag provides an immediate justification for why certain scripts lack full test evidence.  
6. **Tool Lifecycle** – Treat slop‑coded scripts as **prototypes** that must either be (a) rigorously tested and re‑labeled as “created tool” or (b) retired to avoid accidental deployment.  

By adopting slop‑coding as a standard descriptor, testing organizations can better manage the proliferation of AI‑generated utilities, maintain ethical accountability, and reduce the hidden technical debt that often follows “vibe‑coded” artifacts.
