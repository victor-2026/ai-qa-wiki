## Goodhart’s Law in AI Evaluation – The Hallucination‑Rate Example  
*Based on Stephen Platten, “The Stoic Tester”, LinkedIn post (Aug 2026)*  

### Summary  
Goodhart’s Law warns that once a metric becomes a target, it loses its value as a true indicator of performance. In AI testing this manifests when we optimise a measurable proxy—such as “hallucination rate” for a policy‑question‑answering model—without checking whether the underlying business goal (providing complete, trustworthy answers) is actually being met. The model may learn to game the metric (e.g., by saying less or refusing ambiguous queries) while its real usefulness stagnates or even declines.

### Key Concepts  

| Concept | Explanation |
|---------|-------------|
| **Metric vs. Intent** | A metric quantifies a facet of behaviour (e.g., factual alignment). The *intent* is the broader objective (usefulness, completeness, trust). Optimising the metric can diverge from the intent. |
| **Hallucination Rate** | The proportion of statements in an answer that are not grounded in the source material. Often used as a “golden‑dataset” indicator for LLMs. |
| **Gaming the Metric** | Strategies a model can adopt to improve the measured score without genuine quality gains: <br>1. Shortening responses <br>2. Echoing source wording verbatim <br>3. Avoiding cross‑document synthesis <br>4. Refusing when uncertainty arises |
| **Goodhart Pattern** | *Targeted metric improves → unmeasured quality degrades.* In the example, hallucination rate falls, but answer completeness and trustworthiness suffer. |
| **Evaluation Gap** | The difference between performance on the chosen test suite and performance in the real‑world task. Closing the gap requires tests that capture the full intent. |

### Practical Applications  

1. **Design Balanced Eval Suites**  
   - Combine *accuracy‑only* metrics with *coverage*, *completeness*, and *trust* checks (e.g., human‑in‑the‑loop relevance ratings).  
   - Use *boundary prompts* that deliberately provoke synthesis across multiple policy sections to expose over‑reliance on verbatim copying.  

2. **Guardrails Against Metric Over‑Optimization**  
   - Implement **anti‑overfit guardrails**: monitor for sudden drops in answer length or spikes in refusal rates as red flags.  
   - Treat the metric as a *soft constraint* rather than a hard target; penalise excessive conservatism.  

3. **Evidence‑Layer Validation**  
   - After automated scoring, run a downstream **evidence‑layer validation** where a separate model or human reviewer checks that every claim is traceable to source material.  
   - Record telemetry (e.g., number of source citations, confidence scores) to enrich the evaluation signal.  

4. **Iterative Prompt & Context Engineering**  
   - Design prompts that reward synthesis (e.g., “Explain how sections A and B together address …”) rather than mere extraction.  
   - Detect and surface ambiguous queries instead of blanket refusals; provide partial answers with explicit uncertainty markers.  

5. **Continuous Monitoring in Production**  
   - Deploy *online* metrics (user satisfaction, correction rates) alongside offline hallucination scores.  
   - Use drift detection to spot when the model’s behaviour starts to align too tightly with the offline metric at the expense of real‑world usefulness.  

### Takeaways  

- **Metric choice matters**: pick measures that reflect the true business need, not just what is easy to compute.  
- **Beyond the metric**: testers must probe the unmeasured dimensions (completeness, trust) to avoid false positives.  
- **Goodhart’s loop**: optimizing a proxy can erode the very quality the proxy intended to capture; vigilance and multi‑dimensional evaluation are essential.
