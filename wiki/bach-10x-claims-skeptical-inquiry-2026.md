## Summary  
James Bach’s “10×‑your‑work” post (Satisfice, 2026) challenges the hype around AI‑driven productivity spikes. Rather than accepting bold claims at face value, Bach proposes an eight‑question skeptical inquiry that uncovers hidden assumptions, measurement flaws, and trade‑offs. The framework helps testers, managers, and buyers separate genuine process improvements from marketing hype and ensures that any AI‑enabled change is evaluated holistically—considering both the **gross** gains and the **net** costs.

## Key Concepts  

| # | Question | Why it matters |
|---|----------|----------------|
| 1 | **What exactly changed?** | Identifies whether the alteration is trivial, replicable, or context‑specific. |
| 2 | **How was it measured?** | Exposes baseline quality, sampling bias, or insufficient data that can inflate gains. |
| 3 | **Are we measuring anything real?** | Distinguishes fixing a broken process from true multiplicative improvement; algorithmic effects can be non‑linear. |
| 4 | **What was sacrificed?** | Highlights hidden costs: technical debt, reduced testability, safety risks, skill erosion, or morale impacts. |
| 5 | **Did anyone test the claim?** | Calls for independent verification to guard against self‑served anecdotes. |
| 6 | **Gross vs. net effect?** | Accounts for new expenses (tools, compute, prompt‑engineering, verification effort) that erode the headline figure. |
| 7 | **How many were affected?** | Scales the claim; a “100×” boost for a single developer on a tiny task is less significant than a modest gain across a whole team. |
| 8 | **Will it work again?** | Probes robustness across data, domains, and objectives; AI may excel in one niche while regressing elsewhere. |

### “Produce, not necessarily productive”  
AI expands the *quantity* of attempts (tokens, drafts, variations) but does not guarantee that the *quality* or *value* of those attempts rises proportionally. True productivity gains require optimizing the entire loop—generation, judgment, and cleanup—not just raw output rate.

## Practical Applications  

1. **Vendor Evaluation** – Use the 8‑question checklist when reviewing AI‑testing tools or services (e.g., AI‑driven test‑case generation). Document baseline conditions, measurement methods, and net cost calculations before signing contracts.  
2. **Internal Process Audits** – Teams can run the inquiry on any internal AI pilot (e.g., AI‑assisted exploratory testing) to surface hidden trade‑offs and decide whether to scale.  
3. **Metrics Design** – When defining KPIs for AI‑augmented testing, ensure they capture *net* outcomes (defect detection rate, cycle‑time reduction) rather than raw token counts or “tests written per hour.”  
4. **Training & Coaching** – Teach testers to view AI as a *productivity aid* that requires judgment. Emphasize prompt‑engineering, result validation, and the cost of “AI slop” (irrelevant or low‑quality output).  
5. **Risk Management** – Incorporate question 4 and 8 into risk registers: track technical debt, safety implications, and regression risk when AI is swapped into a new domain.  

By embedding this skeptical inquiry into QA governance, organizations can avoid the “AI‑first” trap, make evidence‑based buying decisions, and ensure that any claimed “10×” improvement survives real‑world scrutiny.
