Based on the sources, both Google and Meta are shifting their evaluation criteria from manual coding skills to **"AI Fluency,"** which focuses on how effectively a candidate can collaborate with artificial intelligence to solve technical problems.

Google has launched a pilot program for Software Engineering and QA roles that allows candidates to use the **Gemini** AI assistant during interviews. The criteria have moved away from "writing code from scratch" to a **"Code Comprehension"** model, which evaluates:

- **Tool Integration:** Candidates are evaluated on their ability to use a variety of models (from **OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google**) within a live development environment.
- **Real-World Application:** The focus is on solving actual product tasks using AI agents rather than reciting memorized algorithms.

**Common "AI Fluency" Markers**

The sources highlight specific markers that both companies (and others like Canva and Cognition) use to distinguish high-performing candidates in this new format:

- **Ownership & Verification:** The most critical marker is whether a candidate takes "ownership" of the code. **"Passive agreement"**—accepting AI-generated code without checking it—is considered a primary reason for failure.
- **Edge Case Testing:** Candidates are specifically judged on their ability to prompt the AI to account for, or manually verify, **edge cases** (boundary conditions) that the AI might have missed.
- **AI Management:** Rather than just being a "coder," the candidate is evaluated as a manager of AI agents, ensuring the final product is functional and optimized.

What are the common markers used to evaluate AI fluency?

Common markers used to evaluate **AI fluency** focus on a candidate's ability to act as a manager of AI tools rather than just a manual coder. Companies like Google and Meta have shifted their rubrics to assess how effectively an engineer can direct, critique, and verify AI-generated output.

The most common markers for AI fluency include:

**1. Rigorous Output Validation**

This is frequently cited as the most critical signal. Interviewers watch whether a candidate **critically reviews every line** of AI-generated code or accepts it blindly.

- **Catching Mistakes:** Identifying "plausible-but-wrong" logic, such as off-by-one errors, missing null checks, or incorrect standard library usage, is a primary marker.
- **Testing Habit:** A high-fluency marker is the "prompt, review, run, confirm" cycle—running code after every generation to catch cascading failures early.
- **Avoiding "Passive Agreement":** The fastest way to fail is to "rubber-stamp" AI output without verification.

**2. Prompt Engineering and Granularity**

Interviewers evaluate the quality and specificity of the instructions given to the AI.

- **Contextual Clarity:** Strong candidates provide the AI with specific data structures, constraints, and quality requirements (e.g., thread safety or type hints).
- **Incremental Instructions:** High-fluency candidates use **prompt granularity**, asking for small, reviewable slices of logic (like a specific function) rather than trying to "one-shot" an entire solution.

**3. Technical Ownership and Control**

A major marker is whether the human is leading the reasoning or if the AI is driving the decisions.

- **Architectural Decisions:** Candidates are expected to make high-level decisions (like choosing BFS over DFS) **before** opening the AI chat.
- **Redirection:** If the AI drifts into a suboptimal or incorrect approach, the candidate must be able to recognize it and steer it back to their original plan.

**4. Debugging AI Suggestions**

Rather than just "writing" code, candidates are judged on their **"code-auditing"** skills.

- **Hypothesis-Driven Debugging:** High-fluency engineers form a hypothesis about a bug and use the AI for a "second-pass diagnosis" rather than just asking the AI to "fix it".
- **Handling Hallucinations:** The ability to navigate and correct an AI model when it provides outdated syntax or hallucinates a library function.

**5. Strategic Delegation**

Knowing **what** to give to the AI is a marker of seniority and fluency.

- **Mechanics vs. Logic:** Using AI for "boring" or mechanical tasks like boilerplate, test scaffolding, and syntax lookups while personally handling complex business logic and edge case identification.

**6. Communication and Narration**

Because AI generates code faster than a human can explain it, candidates must narrate their collaboration.

- **Narration of Intent:** Explaining **why** they are prompting the AI for a specific task and **what they expect** to see in the output.
- **Critique and Reasoning:** Explaining why they are rejecting or modifying certain parts of the AI's suggestion.

How can I demonstrate technical ownership of AI-generated code?

Demonstrating **technical ownership** of AI-generated code is the most critical factor in passing modern "AI Fluency" interviews at companies like Google and Meta. Ownership means acting as the **driver, not a passenger**, and treating the AI as a "fast but error-prone junior developer".

To demonstrate technical ownership effectively, you should follow these four strategies:

1. Own the Decision Before the Prompt

The single biggest mistake is prompting the AI before you have a plan.

- **The "3-5 Minute Rule":** Spend the first several minutes understanding the problem and outlining your architectural approach (e.g., choosing a hashmap over an array or BFS over DFS) **without touching the AI**.
- **Frame the Problem:** State your intended approach to the interviewer first. This ensures you are the one making technical decisions, while the AI merely executes them.
- **Direct the Workflow:** Don't ask the AI "How should I solve this?". Instead, give it specific, granular instructions like "Implement a trie class that supports insert and prefix search".

2. Implement the "Audit Mindset"

Interviewers are looking for a **"prompt, review, run, confirm"** cycle. Technical ownership is proven when you find the errors the AI inevitably makes.

- **Identify "Plausible-but-Wrong" Code:** AI often misses edge cases, such as off-by-one errors or thread-safety issues. Catching these shows you are in control.
- **Reject Suggestions Out Loud:** If the AI proposes a suboptimal or incorrect pattern, explain why you are rejecting it (e.g., "This uses O(n²) when we need O(n log n)").
- **The "Line-by-Line" Review:** Never "rubber-stamp" or blindly paste large blocks of code. Be prepared to explain the purpose and complexity of every single line the AI generates.

3. Delegate Strategically

High-performing candidates use AI for the "boring" parts and keep the "hard" parts for themselves.

- **Mechanics to AI:** Use the tool for boilerplate, syntax lookups (e.g., "What is the Python syntax for a frozen dataclass?"), input parsing, and test scaffolding.
- **Logic to Yourself:** Personally handle algorithm selection, trade-off analysis, and integration logic.
- **Verification Habit:** Run unit tests after every meaningful change to catch "cascading failures" early.

4. Narrate Your Hierarchy

Since AI produces code faster than you can explain it, continuous **narration of intent** is the primary signal of ownership.

- **Narrate Before, Not After:** Tell the interviewer _what_ you are about to ask for and _why_ you expect the AI to provide it.
- **Explain the Result:** Once the code is generated, summarize what it does back to the interviewer to prove you have mentally "digested" it.
- **Defend the Code:** If the interviewer challenges a line of AI-generated code, you must be able to justify it or adapt it as if you had written it yourself.

What is the 3-5 minute rule for architectural decisions?

The **3-5 minute rule** is a strategic guideline for modern "AI-enabled" technical interviews at companies like Meta and Google. It dictates that a candidate should spend the first **3 to 5 minutes** of the session understanding the problem and outlining their architectural approach **without touching the AI assistant**.

**Core Principles of the Rule**

- **Think Before Prompting:** You should spend these initial minutes clarifying requirements, defining input/output formats, and choosing high-level algorithms (e.g., choosing BFS over DFS or a hashmap over an array).
- **Establish Ownership:** The rule is designed to prove that **you** are the driver of the solution. By outlining your approach first, you ensure that the AI is used for mechanical implementation rather than the actual "thinking" or decision-making process.
- **Avoid the "Answer Machine" Trap:** Pasting a problem statement into an AI immediately often leads to a "passive agreement" failure mode, where the candidate blindly follows whatever architecture the AI suggests without being able to defend it.

**How to Execute It in an Interview**

1. **Narrate the Intent:** Tell the interviewer explicitly: "I'm going to take a couple of minutes to read through this and make sure I understand the requirements before I start".
2. **Define the Approach:** Once you have a plan, state it clearly to the interviewer (e.g., "I intend to solve this using a backtracking approach optimized with a bitmask").
3. **Use AI for Execution:** Only after this phase should you open the AI chat to delegate specific, granular tasks like writing boilerplate, syntax lookups, or implementing the specific algorithm you have already selected.

**Why It Matters for "AI Fluency"**

The rule addresses the first phase of the modern technical interview: **Problem Decomposition**. Interviewers use these first few minutes to evaluate your independent analytical thinking. Candidates who skip this rule often struggle when the AI suggests a suboptimal or incorrect pattern, as they have no original technical baseline to steer the AI back to.

What common AI bugs should I look for during validation?

During the validation phase of an AI-assisted interview, you should approach the code with an **"audit mindset,"** treating the output as a draft from a fast but fallible junior developer. Interviewers are specifically watching for your ability to identify **"plausible-but-wrong"** patterns that look correct at a glance but fail under scrutiny.

Common AI bugs to look for include:

1. Logical and Algorithmic Errors

- **Boundary and Off-by-One Errors:** These are among the most frequent AI mistakes, particularly in loops or data structure traversals.
- **Incorrect Conditionals:** AI models often produce incorrect if-statements or place them in the wrong order, which can break the intended logic.
- **Algorithmic Optimality:** AI frequently suggests "common" solutions (like O(n²)) even when a more efficient approach (like O(n log n)) is feasible and required for the task.
- **Recursion Depth:** For complex problems, AI-generated solutions may hit maximum recursion depth limits because they fail to account for large input sizes.

2. Input and State Management Issues

- **Empty and Invalid Inputs:** AI often misses checks for null values, empty strings, or zero, which are standard edge cases in production-grade code.
- **Data Model Assumptions:** The model may make incorrect assumptions about your data structures or "hallucinate" non-existent library functions and method signatures.
- **Type Casting Errors:** Look for issues where types are cast incorrectly (e.g., casting an int to a double when the rest of the system expects an int), which can lead to subtle bugs or system failures.
- **Duplicate Handling:** AI-generated logic may fail to correctly handle or reject duplicate inputs, such as repeated guesses in a game or redundant entries in a list.

3. Concurrency and System Environment Bugs

- **Thread Safety and Race Conditions:** AI is notoriously unreliable in concurrent domains; it may provide code that compiles and passes basic tests but fails under real-world concurrency due to a lack of proper locking or synchronization.
- **Unstable Time Sources:** A classic AI bug is using `time.time()`, which can jump backward during system clock adjustments, for features like rate limiters where `time.monotonic()` is required.
- **Environment-Specific Failures:** AI code may struggle with domain-specific edge cases like **timezone conversions**, floating-point precision, or Unicode normalization.

4. Semantic and Contextual Errors

- **Structurally Perfect but Semantically Wrong:** Code may be syntactically correct and follow the requested format but contain "impossible" values because the model lacks domain grounding (e.g., treating a percentage as a raw decimal or vice versa).
- **Regression Failures:** When fixing one part of the code, the AI might break a previously working feature; you must re-run the entire test suite to catch these "silent killers".
- **Inconsistent Naming:** AI output may ignore existing naming conventions or architectural contracts established in other parts of the multi-file codebase.

2. Practice "Prompt Granularity"

Senior-level engineers write specific, information-rich prompts rather than broad, "one-shot" requests.

- **Granular Slices:** Direct the AI to implement **one small slice of logic at a time** (like a single function) so you can review it line-by-line.
- **Include Constraints:** A high-ownership prompt includes specific requirements such as **thread safety, type hints, and explicit data structures**.
- **Avoid Giant Pastes:** Requesting and pasting 100+ lines of code at once is a major red flag; it signals that you are no longer in control of the implementation.

3. Implement an "Audit Mindset" during Validation

In these rounds, **verification is the test**. Interviewers are watching to see if you catch the subtle errors AI inevitably makes.

- **Identify "Plausible-but-Wrong" Code:** Be on high alert for logical errors that look correct at a glance, such as **off-by-one errors, incorrect conditionals, or missing null checks**.
- **Domain-Specific Verification:** Demonstrate seniority by catching environmental bugs; for example, if an AI uses `time.time()` for a rate limiter, correct it to `time.monotonic()` to handle system clock adjustments.
- **Reject Suboptimal Code Out Loud:** If the AI proposes an O(n²) solution when O(n log n) is required, explain why you are rejecting that approach to the interviewer.

4. Strategic Delegation

Ownership is proven by knowing **what** to give to the AI and what to keep for yourself.

- **AI for "Mechanical" Tasks:** Use the assistant for **boilerplate, syntax lookups, test scaffolding, and repetitive input parsing**.
- **Human for "Logic" Tasks:** Personally handle the **algorithm selection, trade-off analysis, and edge case identification**.

5. Continuous Narration of Intent

Since AI can generate code faster than a human can explain it, you must narrate your collaboration to keep the interviewer in the loop.

- **Narrate Before, Not After:** Tell the interviewer what you are about to ask the AI for and **why**.
- **Explain the Result:** Once the code is generated, verbally summarize what it does to prove you have mentally digested the output.
- **Defend Decisions:** If an interviewer challenges a line of AI-generated code, you must be able to justify it or adapt it as if you had written every character yourself.

## Главный маркер оценки на таких интервью

Во всех этих компаниях действует одно негласное правило: **ИИ — это ваш инструмент, а не замена мозгу**.

Самый быстрый способ завалить такое интервью — проявить **«пассивное согласие»** (когда ИИ генерирует код с неочевидным багом или избыточной сложностью $O(N^2)$, а кандидат бездумно соглашается и запускает его). Интервьюеры оценивают качество ваших промптов, критическое мышление при чтении сгенерированного кода и то, как именно вы строите стратегию тестирования для проверки ИИ.
