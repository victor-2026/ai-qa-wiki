# Michael Bolton — Systems thinking, constraints, and AI-generated code (LinkedIn, 2026-08-28)

Source: LinkedIn post by Michael Bolton (1st connection), developsense.com / rapid-software-testing.com
Original text pasted by Victor; scoop from Bolton's own comments.

---

Many years ago, mostly through Jerry Weinberg, James Bach, and Mark Federman, I started studying general systems thinking.

Let's start here: a system is a set of things in meaningful relationship to each other. Change one of those things and one of those relationships, and other things in the system must change if the system is to remain stable. All this requires control, which in turn requires observation. Otherwise the system explodes or collapses — eventually.

In a system that seems safe and stable, consequences of some change don't necessarily manifest immediately. When no one is looking sufficiently closely at the system, effects of change might not be noticed until they cause significant trouble. Effects are harder to recognise and anticipate when no one has attempted to perturb the system or its inputs to find problems about it. That applies not only to products, but also to the processes by which they're developed.

These days, people are very excited about inserting new tools and approaches into software development. Code can be generated at astonishing rates — so quickly, some claim, that it can't be evaluated by humans.

Those same people, so it seems, believe that constraints — things that slow down the output from a system — are intrinsically bad. They're not. Constraints allow us to regulate things going into a system so it doesn't become overwhelmed. Constraints can regulate output, too, so what's coming out of one system doesn't overwhelm other systems, including those in which we are elements ourselves.

When you're not clear on the behavior of a system and the potential problems about it, the time and effort you need to obtain that awareness may look like a "bottleneck".

Let's remember that bottles have necks to regulate output to something observable and manageable. This is why wine doesn't come to your table in a bucket.

Review — examining something and considering its effects — is optional. Testing to look for trouble is optional. Critical thinking is optional too, including critical thinking about products, processes, testing, and review.

The consequences of not thinking critically aren't necessarily knowable immediately. Trouble we experience with software today is a consequence of decisions that were made months and years and decades ago.

Dismissing or marginalising review, testing, and systemic risk — as some pundits and commenters seem to be doing these days — will have consequences later. Luckily for them, they'll probably have moved on, leaving the rest of us to suffer or clean up the mess.

If we don't want that, we'll need to remember the need for constraints and regulation in systems, and we'll need to speak up for them.

I'll take my wine from the bottle, thank you.
