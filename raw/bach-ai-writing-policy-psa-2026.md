## PSA: Don't Say You Use AI for Writing

The author writes his blog posts himself. His policy: "I will often use AI in the role of a *thinking partner* to help me think about writing — but the writing I do is my own." Reason: **it would be a mistake to claim AI authorship, and it would be a falsehood.** An untrue or misleading claim is a scandal waiting to happen (like the researcher who claimed ChatGPT as co-author — ridiculed). Bad reasoning to adopt instead: no one else will know / it's more efficient / my writing is bad anyway / AI can think better than me anyway. Authorship is a statement about who is responsible: a person -- not a tool -- is responsible for the words.

Comment by Josh Kautha: the fundamental error in treating AI as author is the same as treating it as manager — tools should not be creators of human products; framing determines responsibility and accountability.

## Humans In The Way

The "morality" of responsible authors — short post announcing the podcast series "Humans In The Way" (YouTube) and linking to the **5-point AI writing policy** at https://www.satisfice.com/ai-policy:
1. **I am the author.** AI may help me think, but I write, and my name is on it.
2. Editing/paraphrasing AI prose is still a form of authorship if enough has changed; full sections lifted verbatim are not mine.
3. If I contribute nothing but prompts, the credit (and blame) is not mine.
4. I will not impersonate anyone, I will not claim AI output as mine.
5. I disclose substantive AI involvement when it's tricky (ghostwriters, etc.) — and always flag AI-only content.
